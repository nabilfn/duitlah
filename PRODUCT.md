# Product

## Register

product

## Platform

web

## Users

Primary: Malaysian university students, young professionals, couples, housemates, friend groups, travellers, and small social groups, roughly 18–35. Secondary: families, freelancers, small teams, and event groups. The core context is social — most sessions involve money moving between people, not just tracking a single person's spending. The flagship moment (Bagilah) happens on a phone at a restaurant table right after a meal, so speed and low-friction input matter as much as accuracy.

## Product Purpose

Duitlah is a personal + shared finance platform. Its flagship feature, Bagilah, lets someone scan a receipt, invite friends by QR/link, let each person claim what they had, and get an exact split that reconciles to the cent against the real receipt total — including tax, service charge, discounts, and rounding. Beyond splitting, Duitlah is a full personal finance app: accounts, transactions, budgets, goals, groups, and (later) forecasting and an AI assistant. Success looks like two feelings: "I finally understand my money" and "splitting money with people is no longer annoying."

## Positioning

A financial application that manages both your money and the money relationships between people. Not another expense tracker, and not another bill-splitting utility — the two functions living together, well, is the product.

## Brand Personality

Premium, intelligent, social. Trustworthy and fast underneath the polish — this is still a money app, so competence has to read before charm does. Youthful without being childish; Malaysian without leaning on stereotypes; financially professional without looking like accounting software. Confident color-blocking is the aesthetic lane: a saturated brand color should carry real surface area (in the spirit of Cash App or Monzo's coral), not sit as a thin accent on an otherwise neutral SaaS shell.

## Anti-references

Do not visually copy MAE, GXBank, Wise, Revolut, or Splitwise — Duitlah should be comparable in polish, not derivative in look. Avoid the generic SaaS dashboard reflex: white card, shadow, rounded-xl, repeated everywhere with no hierarchy. Avoid reading as traditional accounting software (dense tables, spreadsheet energy, no personality). Avoid anything that feels like it's hiding uncertainty or dumbing down a number — this product's credibility rests on the math being exact and shown, not obscured behind a friendly gloss.

## Design Principles

Correctness and security come before visual polish anywhere money is shown or calculated — a beautiful number that's wrong is a failure, full stop. Bagilah is the most memorable moment in the product and earns disproportionate design attention relative to its screen count. Calculations are never a black box: a user should always be able to see why they owe what they owe. UI copy uses human language over financial jargon ("People owe you", not "Outstanding Receivable Balance"). The brand differentiates through confident, deliberate color and hierarchy — not through defaulting to the safest, most generic dashboard pattern available.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

WCAG 2.1 AA as the committed baseline: ≥4.5:1 text contrast (≥3:1 for large text), full keyboard navigation, semantic HTML, visible focus states, screen reader support, and touch targets sized for one-handed mobile use at a restaurant table. Every animation ships a `prefers-reduced-motion` alternative. Status is never conveyed by color alone (paid/unpaid/overdue need an icon or label, not just red/green). Charts and graphs need an accessible text summary alongside the visual.
